  **Research (keep with this item so we don’t re-derive):** Matt Unrath, California Policy Lab at UC Berkeley, January 2021.
  - Brief: [Pushed out by paperwork: Why eligible Californians leave CalFresh](https://capolicylab.org/pushed-out-by-paperwork-why-eligible-californians-leave-calfresh/) ([PDF](https://www.capolicylab.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Pushed-out-by-paperwork-why-eligible-Californians-leave-CalFresh.pdf))
  - Working paper: [Targeting, Screening, and Retention](https://www.capolicylab.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/CalFresh-Working-Paper.pdf) (later version also at [mattunrath.github.io](https://mattunrath.github.io/files/research/Unrath_SNAP.pdf))
  - Press: [more than half of leavers still eligible](https://capolicylab.org/news/new-report-more-than-half-of-california-households-leaving-calfresh-program-are-still-eligible/)

  He linked de-identified CalFresh enrollment to EDD wage records (with CDSS), 2014–19. Official CDSS churn (~10% back in 1 month, ~15% in 90 days) **understates** eligible exits: it misses people who come back **after 90 days** and people who **never return**. Conservatively, **55%** of households leaving still looked income-eligible in the exit month (all quarterly earnings dumped into that month). Spreading earnings across the quarter lands around **70%+** (brief also says 75%). Similar using post-exit wages, so it is not just people leaving because a job was coming. People are **six times more likely to leave in a recertification/reporting month**. Unrath’s reentry table (leavers after 2014): 1 month ~10.5–10.9%, 3 months ~17–18%, 6 months ~30%, 12 months 42.2%, **24 months 52.6%** — about half of leavers are back within two years, so a large share stay off longer or don’t come back in that window. Wage-based eligibility among leavers is **3–5×** the 90-day churn rate. Policy punchline: **lengthen the time between verifications** (federal), not only simplify county paperwork. The 2013 shift from quarterly to semi-annual reporting raised 6-month retention about 10–12 points. Working paper also: for every ineligible household the paperwork **induces** to leave in a reporting month, **two eligible** households also leave (~⅔ of those extra exits). Eligibility sample already drops exits shorter than two months (so 55% is a lower bound on eligible leavers). He measures income eligibility **in the exit month and the next month**, not for every month of a multi-year gap.

  **Caveat vs our grey band:** he studied **all leavers** vs wages. CF18 No return is narrower — households **scheduled that month** who neither completed recert/SAR 7 nor reapplied within 4 months. Orange reapplications are a **lower bound** on paperwork-driven eligible exits; the grey No return band is the population CDSS’s 90-day churn drops.

  **Draft estimate if we ever need a number (not decided):** two composition effects cancel in rough terms — recert/SAR 7 month + not the county-ineligible band pushes eligibility **up** vs all leavers; stripping 4-month returners pushes it **down**. Conservative: **~50%** of No return were likely still income-eligible when they left (Unrath’s 55% after a small “didn’t come back in 4 months” haircut). **55%** is defensible if we don’t haircut a number he already calls conservative. Mechanical floor after assuming every 2–4 month returner was in the eligible 55%: **~45–50%**. Less-conservative / reporting-month induced-exit: **~⅔**. Do not subtract the 24-month reentry rate (52.6%) from 55% — that would wrongly imply almost none of the never-returners were eligible at exit. If used in a lost-benefits sensitivity: **50% of the grey band**, with ⅔ as an upper bound. “Eligible the whole time” is a bit stronger than what he measured; for pricing, the right concept is **eligible when they left**.
